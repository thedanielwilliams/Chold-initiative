Traditional leadership is the missing link in livestock development

The institutions with the deepest proximity to livestock-keeping communities are the ones least involved in designing livestock programmes. That is not a cultural oversight; it is a systems failure with a measurable cost.

There is a pattern in Nigerian livestock programming that anyone who has worked a vaccination campaign in the north will recognise. A national plan is designed in Abuja. A budget is approved. Cold chain is mobilised, teams are deployed, and somewhere in the third week the programme discovers that a particular Emirate is not participating, not out of hostility, but because nobody asked the people who actually convene that community until the launch ceremony.

By then the intervention has become something happening to the community rather than something owned by it. Coverage figures suffer. The next campaign inherits the suspicion. And a line appears in the evaluation report about “community sensitisation challenges.”

Passive engagement is the default, and it is expensive

CHOLD Initiative's analysis of the sector names this plainly as one of eight structural constraints: critical actors and gatekeepers are not strategically engaged in livestock development in Nigeria. Traditional institutions, which hold proximity to and direct influence over rural communities, have only ever been engaged passively.

Passive engagement looks like this. The Emir is invited to declare the programme open. A District Head is given a courtesy briefing. A photograph is taken. Then implementation proceeds through a parallel structure of field officers who have no standing local authority and no ability to resolve a dispute over a grazing route or a herd owner's refusal to allow tagging.

The question is not whether traditional leaders should be respected. It is whether they should be given a defined role, a budget line and a set of indicators, the same things every other implementing partner receives.

What these institutions actually control

In much of Nigeria's livestock belt, traditional institutions retain functional authority over exactly the variables that determine whether a livestock intervention succeeds:

  • Access to grazing land and water points: the resource at the heart of most farmer–pastoralist conflict.
  • Dispute resolution: the first and often only forum for a stolen-cattle claim or a crop-damage complaint.
  • Convening power: the ability to assemble herd owners at short notice during an outbreak.
  • Legitimacy of new practice: whether a new vaccine, a tagging scheme or a digital registry is treated as help or as surveillance.
  • Women's participation: whether female livestock keepers, who often manage small ruminants and poultry, are permitted into a training room.

No amount of technical excellence substitutes for those five things. A perfectly designed Livestock Information Management System that communities will not populate with honest data is a very expensive empty database.

The public health sector already proved this

The strongest evidence for the argument does not come from the livestock sector at all. It comes from polio.

Nigeria's eventual removal from the global polio-endemic list was not achieved by cold chain logistics alone. It was achieved when traditional and religious leadership was brought inside the campaign structure when Emirate councils established health committees, when traditional rulers personally led door-to-door mobilisation in resistant communities, and when state task forces on primary healthcare were co-chaired by people whose authority predated the state.

Members of the CHOLD Initiative board were directly involved in that work. The Emir of Dass chairs the Bauchi State Emirate Councils Committee on Health and co-chairs the State Task Force on Primary Healthcare, and has led door-to-door campaigns targeting zero-dose children. The Emir of Argungu has been a sustained partner in maternal and child health advocacy and routine immunisation across his Emirate. Our founder coordinated the health interface between the Nigeria Governors' Forum and the states through the critical phase of the eradication campaign.

The lesson transfers exactly. Animal health is a community compliance problem before it is a veterinary one.

What active engagement would look like

Moving from passive to active engagement is a design decision, and it is specific. In our model it means:

  • Co-design, not consultation. Community-led livestock development frameworks developed with traditional institutions from the outset: we are targeting 200 or more by 2030.
  • Training, not briefing. Traditional leaders trained in livestock governance and planning so they can hold technical conversations, not just endorse them. Our target is 3,000 leaders engaged.
  • Culturally tailored communication. Radio programming, workshops and community fora built in the language and register communities actually use.
  • An explicit conflict mandate. Integrating traditional institutions into resource management and peacebuilding, rather than treating conflict as a security matter external to livestock programming.
  • A gender clause. Using the same authority to open training rooms and cooperative membership to women livestock keepers.

The target we hold ourselves to is 80% community adoption of modern livestock practices through trusted intermediaries, and a 50% increase in local uptake of veterinary and livestock technologies where leadership endorsement is secured.

The objection, and the answer

The standard objection is that formalising the role of traditional institutions entrenches unelected authority in development delivery. It deserves a serious answer rather than a dismissal.

The answer is that the authority already exists whether a programme engages it or not. The choice is not between engaging traditional leaders and operating in a purely technocratic space; it is between engaging them deliberately, with defined responsibilities and accountability for results, or informally and invisibly, where influence is exercised without any obligation to programme objectives. Formal engagement makes the relationship legible and therefore governable. It also allows a programme to push, credibly, on inclusion, which is far harder to do from outside the room.

Where this sits in our work

Traditional Leader Engagement is one of eight technical capabilities at CHOLD Initiative, and it is deliberately not the last one on the list. It is the capability that determines whether the other seven survive contact with a real community.

Livestock data systems need people willing to be counted. Disease surveillance needs herders willing to report. Supply chain traceability needs producers willing to be traced. Every one of those is a question of trust before it is a question of technology, and in most of rural Nigeria, trust has a custodian.
